01

The corpus

600 tickets · 520 real · 80 injected

Real customer-support tickets from the public Bitext dataset (CC0), stratified onto eight intent classes — then deliberately contaminated with five families of language that break annotation programs.

Agreement statistics are meaningless on easy data. So 80 tickets were injected across five engineered ambiguity families — sarcasm, mixed intents, polite complaints, tone traps, bare fragments — and another 113 Bitext tickets had their sentiment layer re-wrapped (deadpan phrasing over negative facts, and vice versa). Every injection is logged in the augmentation manifest; nothing is smuggled in.

02

Method

calibrate → gate → label → review → audit
  1. Calibration first.

    48 seeded-difficult tickets, all three annotators, adjudicated live with the QA lead. R1’s calibration log wrote the first draft of every v2 rule.

  2. A gate before production.

    ≥ 85% agreement with the adjudicated calibration set before touching real work. In round 1 Marcus (81%) and Tom (77%) failed the gate and were retrained; after the v2 revision all three re-certified at 98%. The gate, not seniority, decides who labels.

  3. Blind triple overlap.

    180 tickets — gold, calibration and 60 at random — labeled independently by all three. Nobody sees a teammate’s label; there is nothing to converge on but the text.

  4. Rotated single labels in production.

    The remaining 420 tickets split evenly across the crew (140 each per round) — cheap, fast, and exactly where tier-2 sampling earns its keep.

  5. Tier-2 senior review.

    All 420 production tickets plus 29 flagged overlap cases pass the QA lead; disagreements return as rework with a written cause.

  6. Tier-3 audit.

    A stratified sample of 117 final labels re-derived independently — 21 against gold, 96 from the v2 rules with no knowledge of the crew’s answers.

  7. Version the instructions.

    v1 → calibration evidence → v2 (five rule changes, changelogued, each with the ticket IDs that forced it). Guidelines are code; they get reviews and releases.

Is the gain real?

Two independent checks. The round-wise 95% bootstrap CIs for Fleiss’ κ are disjoint ([0.783, 0.880] vs [0.928, 0.980]); and a paired bootstrap over the same 180 items — the honest test, since rounds share tickets — puts Δκ at +0.120, 95% CI [0.072, 0.169], clear of zero.

Can the math be trusted?

The statistics engine (src/agreement.py) is written from scratch and self-tests on every build: Cohen’s κ matches scikit-learn exactly; Krippendorff’s α matches NLTK under nominal and ordinal distance in both rounds; Fleiss reproduces the canonical 0.210 worked example.

Where it still hurts

R2 worst class is cancellation, κ = 0.91 — every class cleared 0.90. Residual risk lives in sarcasm sentiment (unanimous 80%): intent is solved; tone is merely contained.

Reading: R1’s average hid a localized failure — cancellation κ = 0.58 and refund 0.79, the two classes v1’s tone-first rule misrouted. Both cleared 0.90 in R2.

Reading: mixed-intent tickets went from 12% unanimous to 94% — the single largest family repair. Small counts are stated on the chart; slices are diagnostic, not inferential.

Reading: high agreement with the gold set — accuracy, not just consistency. The R2 cluster at 0.96–0.99 is what the tier-3 audit then confirmed on unseen samples.

04

Anatomy of the disagreement

what actually broke

Round 1’s top confusion pairs all live in the money triad: cancellation↔refund (5 + 5 cases between one pair of annotators alone) and billing↔refund (3). The cause was structural, not personal — two v1 rules:

  • Rule 4 — “tone first.” Any anger was routed to feedback_complaints before intent was read. Furious refund demands became complaints.
  • Rule 5 — “politeness ⇒ neutral feedback.” Courteous delivery failures were filed as neutral. Sarcasm, being polite-shaped, sailed straight through.

Version 2 replaced tone-first with a money-movement test, gave sarcasm and politeness explicit handling, and separated asking about a policy from demanding it. Round 2 fell to five scattered mismatches for the same annotator pair — no systematic pair left to fix.

The tone trap — the most important negative result in this program

75%unanimous in R1
yet only
25%matched the intended label

On the 16 engineered tone traps, all three annotators agreed on three-quarters of tickets — and three-quarters of those agreements were wrong. Perfect consensus, systematically mistaken: the textbook case for scoring crews against a gold set, not against each other. After the revision, tone-trap accuracy reached 93.8% for every persona; κ and gold accuracy moved together, which is the only kind of agreement worth paying for.

“Agreement ≠ correctness; the rule, not the annotators, was wrong.”

— guidelines/annotation_guidelines_v2.md, revision table, change C1 · echoed in guidelines/CHANGELOG.md (“DELETED the tone-first rule”)

The smell the trend chart caught: in round 1, κ climbed within the round (0.77 → 0.87) — the crew was out-learning the instructions. Round 2 is flat and high: the labels stopped depending on when they were produced.

05

Quality gates

annotator → senior → auditor

What tier-2 sent back

28 of 600 tickets (4.7%) were returned at senior review — 2.4% intent / 4.5% sentiment among production labels. Four real cases, verbatim from the rework log:

  • GF-0204 — “how do I see your money back policy?” First pass demanded refund; review read a policy question. refund → other
  • GF-0291 — sarcasm the majority missed twice; the one case where consensus lost to a careful reader. pos → neg
  • GF-0128 — invoice download filed as feedback. feedback → billing
  • GF-0090 — “damn invoice”: annoyance under the threshold. neu → neg

A rework rate is a steering instrument, not a grade: under ~20% the instructions are basically sound; over 30% the instructions — not the crew — are the defect. Ours closed at 4.7%.

What v1 would have cost

Counterfactual — replaying round-1 production labels against the v2 rulebook: 4.8% of intents were misrouted and 14.5% of sentiments wrong. On a 420-ticket run that is ≈ 20 intents routed to the wrong team and ≈ 61 tickets carrying the wrong emotional read — every week, invisible to κ alone.

08

Provenance & limits

read before citing
  • The annotators are simulations. Priya, Marcus and Tom are seeded engines in src/annotators.py with documented failure modes (skimming, tone-bias, literalism) modeled on patterns every QA lead will recognize from real crews. Their disagreement ceilings are designed, and the design is printed in the source. This page measures process; it does not claim human psychometrics.
  • Every statistic is recomputed from raw labels. This HTML was rendered from results/metrics.json (sha-256 d25ec5d0cc10c878…) plus the annotation CSVs. Nothing was typed by hand; prose numbers are template variables.
  • One domain, one language. E-commerce support, English, ticket-level intent + 3-level ordinal sentiment. No spans, no hierarchy, no multilingual drift — those change the tooling, not the method.
  • Gold is adjudication, not gospel. The gold set was adjudicated by the QA lead, not external experts; three design labels were overridden by adjudication along the way (e.g. GF-0169, GF-0249). Each override is in the changelog. “High agreement ≠ correctness” cuts both ways — which is why tier-3 re-derives labels independently instead of trusting the set.
  • Data: 520 tickets from the Bitext customer-support set (CC0), stratified to eight intents; 80 injected across five ambiguity families and 113 sentiment re-wraps — every one logged in the augmentation manifest inside src/make_dataset.py.
  • What transfers to a human team: the gates, the tier design, the statistics engine, the calibration-first habit, the changelog discipline. What does not: the specific effect sizes. Plug in the peer sheets and find out.
